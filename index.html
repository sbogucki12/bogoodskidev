Most organizations don't have an AI problem. They have an everything-around-AI problem.

Everyone knows AI matters. The pressure is real, the slides are flashy, the budgets are getting approved. But actually operationalizing this stuff inside a big, complicated organization is a totally different sport.

Here's what tends to happen instead:

  • Pilots that crush the demo and then quietly never scale.
  • Security and data questions that nobody can fully answer, so nothing moves.
  • Governance reviews that turn into bottlenecks instead of guardrails.
  • Nobody quite owns AI services, tooling, or the bill.
  • Tool sprawl, because every team quietly bought or built their own thing.
  • Employees are experimenting way faster than policy can keep up with.
  • Leaders can see the potential, they just can't find the path from "interested" to "in production."

None of these are technology failures. They're enablement failures. The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is everything around it: access, data, cost, policy, security, training, ownership, and the unglamorous job of keeping the thing alive once it's live.

Under the hood: the technical bits I actually care about.

Strategy talk is cheap, and frankly a little suspicious if nobody in the room can explain what a vector embedding is or why the token bill quietly tripled over a long weekend.

So here's a peek inside the toolbox: the nuts, bolts, and occasionally rusty wrenches I reach for when an AI program has to survive honest contact with real users, real data, and a very real security team.

RAG that holds up after the demo

A RAG demo is a lot like a model home: gorgeous, beautifully staged, and nobody actually lives there. The real thing needs plumbing.

  • Sensible chunking, with overlap so ideas don't get guillotined mid-sentence
  • The right embedding model, plus hybrid keyword and vector search
  • Re-ranking, so the best passage wins, not just the closest one
  • Permission-aware retrieval that quietly respects who can see what
  • Clickable citations and groundedness evals, so "it seems fine" stops being the quality bar

LLM gateways and access patterns

A model gateway is the front desk of a wildly popular hotel: everybody's welcome, but everybody checks in first.

  • A central API gateway in front of providers like Amazon Bedrock
  • Least-privilege IAM roles, per-team keys, and quotas
  • An approved-model allow-list
  • Token metering for showback or chargeback, plus rate limits
  • Thoughtful logging, and guardrails on the way in and on the way out

Governance with a fast lane

Good governance is airport security with a PreCheck lane: everybody gets screened, but not everybody has to take their shoes off.

  • Risk-tiered intake with a genuine fast lane for low-risk ideas
  • Data classification baked into the decision, not bolted on afterward
  • Human review where the stakes are genuinely high
  • The NIST AI RMF used as a map, not a cudgel
  • A clear path from "idea" to "approved" to, eventually and gracefully, "retired"

Reading the fine print on AI vendors

Every AI tool swears it's secure, the same way every used car was only ever driven to church on Sundays. So I ask the politely annoying questions.

  • Is our data used for training, and is that promise in the contract or just on the marketing page?
  • How long are prompts retained, and is zero-data-retention on the menu?
  • Where does the data live, and which subprocessors touch it?
  • Is there SSO, audit logging, and a real incident-response story?
  • For public-sector folks: what's the FedRAMP status?

Fundamentals, explained like a human

I genuinely, nerdily love explaining how this stuff works, minus the jargon fog.

  • Supervised versus unsupervised learning
  • What a transformer's attention mechanism is really doing
  • Why a bidirectional encoder like BERT reads a sentence differently than a GPT-style model writes one
  • What tokens and context windows actually cost
  • How embeddings turn meaning into geometry, and why models hallucinate with such breathtaking confidence

The short, plain-English answers to the questions that come up again and again: in meetings, in hallway conversations, and, increasingly, in prompts typed into a chatbot at 11 p.m. by somebody with a deadline and a slightly panicked look in their eyes.

What is enterprise AI enablement?

Enterprise AI enablement is the work of helping large organizations adopt AI safely, usefully, and at scale. It covers the operating model, governed access to large language models, use-case intake and lifecycle, RAG programs, AI software review, cost visibility, and workforce training. Picking a model is maybe ten percent of it. The other ninety percent is making sure the thing is secure, owned, funded, understood, and still running six months after the launch party.

Why do so many enterprise AI pilots never make it to production?

Most AI pilots stall because of everything around the model, not the model itself. Common culprits include unanswered security and data questions, governance reviews that act like bottlenecks, no clear owner for the service or the bill, and no plan for monitoring and support once real users arrive. A pilot proves something can work. Production proves somebody is willing to be responsible for it.

What does a RAG chatbot program need beyond the chatbot itself?

A RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) program needs a governed document ingestion pipeline, sensible chunking and embedding choices, permission-aware retrieval, citations users can verify, evaluation for groundedness and answer quality, cost tracking, and a clear support and ownership model. The chatbot is the storefront. The program is the warehouse, the supply chain, and the people who restock the shelves.

Link to this answer

How should an organization govern access to large language models?

A practical pattern is a central API gateway in front of approved model providers, with least-privilege IAM roles, per-team credentials and quotas, an approved-model allow-list, token metering for cost visibility, logging, and input and output guardrails. The goal is access that is safe enough for the security team and fast enough that employees don't go hunting for unsanctioned workarounds.

Link to this answer

What should you check before approving an AI-enabled software tool?

Before approving an AI tool, confirm whether your data is used to train the vendor's models (and whether that's guaranteed in the contract), how long prompts and outputs are retained, where data is stored and processed, which subprocessors are involved, whether SSO and audit logging are supported, and how the vendor handles security incidents. For public-sector buyers, authorization status such as FedRAMP matters too.

What's under the hood (because I honestly can't help myself)

  • Plain, carefully tuned HTML, CSS, and a light sprinkle of vanilla JavaScript. No framework, no build step, no trackers, no cookies, no third-party requests.
  • A strict Content Security Policy, HSTS, and locked-down permissions headers, because practicing what I preach includes the boring security parts.
  • A self-hosted variable font and right-sized WebP images, so the page loads briskly even on a phone clinging to one bar of signal.
  • Schema.org structured data that wires me, my services, and the FAQ above into a tidy little knowledge graph.
  • An llms.txt and llms-full.txt, plus a robots.txt that rolls out the welcome mat for AI crawlers, so AI assistants can read this site as easily as people can.
